# 29. Hiring, Agencies and Outsourcing

> The hardest hire to make is the one you can't evaluate. This chapter is about buying marketing
> when you don't know how to assess marketing — which is the position almost every technical
> founder is in, and the reason so many first marketing hires fail.

---

## 29.1 When to hire, and when not to

**Don't hire a marketer to find product-market fit.** They can't, and it isn't their job. If you
don't yet know who buys and why, that's founder work — see
[chapter 20](20-founder-led-sales.md).

**Do hire when** you know what works and can't do enough of it. That's the honest signal: a
repeatable motion that's constrained by your hours rather than by your understanding.

The failure pattern is consistent and expensive: a founder who dislikes marketing hires someone to
own it entirely, can't evaluate their work, gives no strategic direction, and concludes eighteen
months later that "marketing didn't work." The hire was set up to fail on day one.

**If you hire, you must stay involved.** Not doing the work — but able to judge it, and providing
the positioning ([chapter 1](01-positioning-and-audience.md)) that the marketer executes against.

---

## 29.2 Which profile

For a first hire, **generalist** is almost always right. You need someone who can write, ship a
landing page, run an email sequence, read analytics and talk to customers. Specialists are for
when you have a machine to optimise.

The archetypes and when each fits:

- **Generalist / full-stack marketer** — the default first hire. Breadth over depth, comfortable with ambiguity.
- **Content marketer** — right when your motion is genuinely content-led and you know it.
- **Product marketer** — positioning, messaging, launches, competitive work. Right slightly later, and often the highest-leverage second hire for technical products.
- **Growth marketer** — experimentation and funnel optimisation. Needs a funnel with traffic to optimise; premature at low volume ([chapter 18](18-landing-pages-and-conversion.md)).
- **DevRel** — a different discipline entirely. See [chapter 5](05-developer-marketing-and-devrel.md), including the honest note about how the function fared in recent layoffs.

**Evaluating without expertise.** You can't assess their craft, but you can assess their thinking:

- **Ask them to critique your current marketing** in the interview. Specific, prioritised criticism is the signal. Vague enthusiasm is the anti-signal.
- **Ask what they'd stop doing.** Good marketers subtract.
- **Ask for something they wrote** and read it properly.
- **Give a small paid trial project.** A day or two of real work tells you more than four interviews.
- **Ask how they'd know if it was working.** If the answer has no numbers in it, keep looking.

- [MKT1](https://mkt1.co/) — Emily Kramer and Kathleen Estreich, both former first marketing hires. The most directly useful resource in this chapter, specifically on scoping and making that hire.
- [Lenny's Newsletter](https://www.lennysnewsletter.com/) — extensive interview-based coverage of first marketing hires and how they actually went.
- [First Round Review](https://review.firstround.com/) — deep archive on early marketing hires.

## 29.3 Hiring for developer marketing specifically

A marketer who has only sold to non-technical buyers will struggle, and it's usually not their
fault — the playbook they've learned is actively counterproductive here. Gated ebooks, lead-nurture
sequences and "book a demo" walls repel the audience.

**What to look for:** genuine technical curiosity, willingness to read documentation, and the
ability to write something an engineer would respect. They don't need to code. They do need to be
able to tell when something is technically wrong.

**A useful test:** ask them to explain your product back to you. Someone who can absorb a technical
product quickly and re-explain it clearly is doing the core job of the role in front of you.

- [Developer Marketing Does Not Exist](https://www.developermarketing.com/) — Adam DuVander. Short, opinionated, and the right thing to hand a candidate from a non-technical background.
- [awesome-devrel](https://github.com/devrelcollective/awesome-devrel) — community-sourced list of developer-relations resources.
- [SlashData / Developer Nation](https://www.developernation.net/) and the [Stack Overflow Developer Survey](https://survey.stackoverflow.co/) — the two largest recurring developer population surveys; useful for calibrating claims a candidate makes about "what developers want."
- [Developer Marketing Alliance](https://www.developermarketing.io/) and [Product Marketing Alliance](https://www.productmarketingalliance.com/) — communities running salary and benchmark surveys, useful for setting a band.

## 29.4 Evaluating agencies without marketing expertise

Agencies can be genuinely good and the market has a lot of noise in it. You can filter most of the
noise without being a marketer.

**Red flags:**

- **Guaranteed results.** Nobody can guarantee a ranking, a citation or a conversion rate. Anyone who does is either lying or planning to game a metric.
- **They won't tell you specifically what they'll do**, only what you'll get.
- **Their own marketing is bad.** Look at their site, their content, their search presence.
- **They can't name a client you can call.**
- **Deliverables are measured in volume** — "twenty blog posts a month" — rather than outcome.
- **Long lock-in with no exit.** Six months is reasonable for SEO. Twelve with no break clause is not.
- **They won't explain their reporting methodology**, or the report is a screenshot of a dashboard you already own.

**Green flags:** they ask about your business before pitching, they tell you what they *won't* do,
they're willing to start small, and they push back on something you said.

**Always:** own your accounts. Analytics, ad accounts, domain, CMS and repositories in your name,
with the agency granted access. Agencies who insist on owning them are creating switching costs at
your expense.

- [Google Search Central: do you need an SEO?](https://developers.google.com/search/docs/fundamentals/do-i-need-seo) — Google's own guide, including what to ask an SEO agency and the warning signs. Free, short, and the single most useful thing to read before signing anything.

## 29.5 Fractional and contract

**Fractional CMOs** work when you need strategic direction and can execute yourself, or already
have junior people who need steering. They fail when hired to *do* the work — a day a week is not
enough hands.

**Contractors and freelancers** are frequently better value than an agency for a specific
deliverable: one landing page, one video, one technical article. The trade is that you have to
manage them, which means writing a real brief.

**A brief that works** contains: who it's for, what it must accomplish, what already exists, what
success looks like, what's out of scope, and who approves. Most contractor relationships fail on
the last two.

- [Contract Killer](https://stuffandnonsense.co.uk/contract-killer/) — Andy Clarke's freely reusable plain-language contract template. Long-standing, widely adapted, and vastly better than no contract.

## 29.6 What not to outsource

Some things lose their value when someone else does them:

- **Founder-led sales**, until the motion is proven ([chapter 20](20-founder-led-sales.md)).
- **Your own technical voice.** Ghostwritten engineering content reads as ghostwritten to engineers.
- **Community presence.** An outsourced community manager posting as the founder is a trust problem waiting to happen, and it's the kind of thing that surfaces publicly.
- **Customer research.** The value is in *you* hearing it ([chapter 1](01-positioning-and-audience.md)).
- **Positioning.** An agency can facilitate the work. It cannot decide what you are.

---

## 29.7 If you only do five things

1. Don't hire a marketer to find product-market fit. That's your job.
2. Hire a generalist first, and stay involved enough to judge the work.
3. Ask every candidate and agency to critique your current marketing, specifically.
4. Own every account in your own name, always.
5. Write real briefs, including what's out of scope and who approves.
