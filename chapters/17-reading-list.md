# 17. Reading List

> A deliberately short list. Everything here is worth your whole attention; nothing is here
> because it's famous. Where a book is dated, it says so — some ideas survive their examples.

---

## Books

**Positioning and messaging**

- **Obviously Awesome** — April Dunford (2019). The right first book for an engineer who built something good and can't explain it. Positioning as a mechanical process rather than a creative act.
- **Sales Pitch** — April Dunford (2023). The follow-up, on saying it out loud. Structures the pitch around the buyer's alternatives, which makes it unusually compatible with how engineers prefer to sell.
- **Demand-Side Sales 101** — Bob Moesta (2020). Jobs to be Done applied to selling. The best sales book for people who dislike selling.

**Pricing**

- **Monetizing Innovation** — Madhavan Ramanujam & Georg Tacke (2016). Still the best first book on pricing. Its examples predate AI; its central discipline — have the willingness-to-pay conversation before you build — does not.
- **Scaling Innovation** — Ramanujam & Hartman (2025). Where the AI-era monetisation framework lives, including the autonomy-versus-attribution test for whether outcome pricing can work at all.
- **Methods of Pricing** — Nick Kolenda. Tactical psychological pricing; the highest-ROI reading for someone about to design a pricing page.

**Growth and business model**

- **Product-Led Growth** — Wes Bush. The canonical PLG text.
- **MAKE: The Indie Maker Handbook** — Pieter Levels. Continuously updated since 2018. Opinionated, occasionally dogmatic, and the most honest account of shipping many small products fast.
- **The Business Value of Developer Relations** — Mary Thengvall (2018). Still the standard text for justifying the function internally, which is the whole job now.
- **Developer Marketing Does Not Exist** — Adam DuVander (2021). Short, and correct about why the conventional toolbox fails on engineers.

**Psychology**

- **Influence** and **Pre-Suasion** — Robert Cialdini. The second is the more underrated.
- **Thinking, Fast and Slow** — Daniel Kahneman. Read with the replication crisis in mind; the framing has aged better than some of the examples.

**Essays worth more than most books**

- [Do Things that Don't Scale](http://paulgraham.com/ds.html) — Paul Graham. The most useful thing ever written about getting your first users.
- [Introducing AX: Why Agent Experience Matters](https://biilmann.blog/articles/introducing-ax/) — Matt Biilmann (2025). The best conceptual anchor for what AI agents do to developer-tool distribution.

## Research and data worth citing

Sources that publish real numbers rather than repackaging each other's:

- [Pew Research Center](https://www.pewresearch.org/) — independent, non-commercial, transparent methodology. Their 2025 study on clicks and AI summaries is the best-sourced data point in the whole AI-search debate.
- [SaaS Capital research](https://www.saas-capital.com/research/) — the only major benchmark publisher that surveys **bootstrapped** companies, which is what most readers of this list actually are.
- [ChartMogul Insights](https://chartmogul.com/insights/) — built on real aggregated subscription data rather than survey self-report.
- [Bessemer Atlas](https://www.bvp.com/atlas) — the highest signal-per-word VC publication on cloud and AI business models.
- [SlashData / Developer Nation](https://www.developernation.net/) — the long-running quantitative survey of developer populations and tool adoption.
- [Google Search Central](https://developers.google.com/search) — first-party, free, and consistently less breathless than the industry writing about it.

## How to read a marketing statistic

A short methodology note, because this list spent a lot of its research budget discovering how
much of this field is repackaged vendor content:

1. **Who paid for it?** A study showing that brand mentions predict AI visibility, published by the company selling brand-mention tracking, is not disqualified — but it is an advertisement with a methodology section.
2. **Is it correlational or causal?** Almost all of it is correlational. Almost all of it is written as though it were causal.
3. **What was the sample, and of what?** "48% of AI Overviews" and "25% of searches" can both be true and describe different universes. Keyword-tracker corpora over-index on question-shaped queries.
4. **Can you reach the primary source?** If a number has been through three blogs, it is folklore. Reddit statistics, Hacker News front-page rates and Product Hunt upvote thresholds are almost entirely folklore of this kind.
5. **Is the underlying data public?** Hacker News, Stack Exchange and GitHub all publish theirs. If a claim about those platforms matters to your decision, compute it yourself — it's an afternoon, and you'll be one of the few people citing something real.
