# 2. Getting Your First Users With No Budget

> This is the part of marketing that has changed least. The tactics turn over constantly; the
> logic doesn't. Getting from zero to your first hundred users is still a manual, unglamorous,
> unscalable process, and every attempt to skip it fails the same way.

---

## 2.1 The pieces that still hold up

Most growth writing ages badly. This doesn't:

- [Do Things that Don't Scale](http://paulgraham.com/ds.html) — Paul Graham. Still the best thing ever written on this problem, and if anything more relevant now that everyone has an AI-generated growth playbook. The core argument — that recruiting users one at a time by hand is not a compromise but the actual job — is unchanged.
- **The unscalable phase is where the product gets good.** Doing support personally, onboarding people by hand, and watching them fail in real time is the highest-bandwidth product research that exists. Founders who automate this too early ship products that are subtly wrong for years.

None of that is controversial. What follows is what has changed underneath it.

---

## 2.2 What "no budget" means differently now

**Distribution is more crowded and more concentrated.** Not long ago, publishing something decent
gave you a reasonable chance of being found. Now the volume of published content is far higher, the
share of searches ending in a click is far lower, and the surfaces that matter are fewer and more
gate-kept.

**But the bar for *quality* is easier to clear than ever.** That sounds contradictory; it isn't.
When generic competent content became free to produce, generic competent content stopped working.
The things that now stand out are exactly the things an engineer can do and a content mill can't:

- **Original data.** Benchmarks you ran. Numbers from your own system. A survey of your users. Nobody can generate this, and it's the single most reliable way to earn citations, links and AI mentions in 2026.
- **Genuine technical depth.** The post that goes three levels deeper than every other post on the topic.
- **A real opinion with your name on it.** Models produce the average of all opinions. The average is not interesting.
- **Showing the actual work** — the failures, the numbers, the decisions you'd make differently.

**The practical implication:** the old advice was "publish consistently." The 2026 version is
"publish something nobody else *could* have published, less often."

## 2.3 Where the first hundred actually come from

In rough order of yield for a technical product:

**1. People who already have the problem, in places they already gather.**
Not "post about your product." Participate genuinely in the communities from
[chapter 8](08-communities-and-social.md) for weeks, answering questions in your problem space,
before you ever link your own thing. This is slow, and it is the single highest-converting
channel available to someone with no audience.

**2. Direct, honest, individual outreach.**
Twenty carefully researched messages to people who visibly have the problem beats two thousand
templated ones — and, unlike the 2018 version of this advice, the templated version is now both
ineffective and legally fraught. See [chapter 10](10-outbound-and-partnerships.md).

**3. Free tools and open source.**
Build something small, genuinely useful, and free, adjacent to your paid thing. This is the most
durable no-budget channel for engineers because it plays to your actual advantage. A useful CLI,
a good calculator, a well-made converter, or an open-source library gets shared on merit.

**4. Writing that only you could write.**
See 2.2. One post with original data outperforms twenty competent explainers.

**5. Launches.**
Real, but late in this list on purpose — a launch converts an audience, it doesn't create one.
See [chapter 7](07-launching.md).

**6. Being where the models look.**
Genuinely new, and genuinely important: being mentioned in the sources that AI assistants read —
community threads, comparison content, documentation, other people's lists — increasingly
determines whether you're the answer when someone asks a model what to use. See
[chapter 4](04-seo-and-ai-search.md).

## 2.4 Build in public

The most reliable audience-building strategy available to an engineer with no budget, and it
works because it inverts the usual problem: instead of needing an audience to launch, the process
of building *creates* the audience.

What makes it work rather than become noise:

- **Share the decisions, not the milestones.** "We chose Postgres over DynamoDB and here's the analysis" is interesting. "Big week ahead!" is not.
- **Share numbers.** Revenue, users, churn, infrastructure costs. Specificity is the entire product.
- **Share failures with the same energy as wins.** This is where trust is actually built, and almost nobody does it.
- **Be consistent for longer than feels reasonable.** The compounding is real and slow.

[Indie Hackers](https://www.indiehackers.com/) remains the best home for this, and its archive of
real revenue numbers is worth reading before you start.

## 2.5 What no longer works

Cut these from any plan you inherited:

- **Answering questions on Stack Overflow.** The channel has collapsed. See [chapter 8](08-communities-and-social.md#stack-overflow-say-the-quiet-part-loudly).
- **Publishing on Medium for distribution.** It's paywalled and its distribution to non-subscribers is a shadow of what it once was. Own your domain; syndicate elsewhere.
- **Quora.** Declined severely as both a traffic source and a community.
- **Facebook groups and Pages.** Organic reach for brand pages has been negligible for years.
- **Mass cold email.** Ineffective and, depending on jurisdiction, unlawful. See [chapter 15](15-privacy-and-compliance.md).
- **Follow/unfollow, auto-DMs, engagement pods.** These worked briefly, then didn't, and now mostly signal that you're not worth following.
- **"Growth hacking" content from the 2014–2018 era generally.** Most of it describes exploiting platform mechanics that have since been closed.

## 2.6 The honest expectations section

Nothing in this chapter produces a hockey stick. The realistic shape of a no-budget start is:

- **Weeks 1–8:** near-zero. You are building the raw material and the relationships.
- **Months 2–4:** the first users arrive individually, by hand, from conversations. Each one takes real effort. This is normal and not a sign of failure.
- **Months 4–12:** if you kept publishing something worth reading and stayed genuinely present in a community or two, compounding starts. Inbound begins to arrive from sources you can't fully trace.

The most common failure isn't picking the wrong channel. **It's quitting the right one at month
three**, which is exactly when it was about to start working.

---

## 2.7 If you only do five things

1. Recruit your first twenty users by hand, individually. Do not automate this.
2. Publish one thing nobody else could have published — your own data, your own numbers.
3. Pick two communities and be genuinely useful in them for two months before mentioning your product.
4. Build one small free thing adjacent to your paid thing.
5. Keep going past month three.
