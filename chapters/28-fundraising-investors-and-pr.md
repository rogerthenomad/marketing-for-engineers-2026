# 28. Fundraising, Investors and Press

> Raising money is a marketing problem with a different audience. Most of what you know about
> customer marketing transfers; the parts that don't are the parts that get founders in
> trouble.

---

## 28.1 How the investor narrative differs

Customers buy a solution to a problem they have today. Investors buy a **claim about the future** —
specifically, that this could be very large. Those are different stories, and a deck that reads
like a product page is the most common failure.

What changes:

- **The market matters more than the product.** Customers don't care how big your market is; investors care about almost nothing else.
- **You are selling the trajectory**, not the current state. Growth rate over absolute numbers.
- **The team slide is real**, not a formality. Early-stage investing is substantially a judgement about people.
- **"Why now" is load-bearing.** Why is this possible or necessary this year and not five years ago? A weak answer here sinks otherwise good pitches.

What stays the same: positioning still has to be crisp, and if you can't explain what you do in a
sentence ([chapter 1](01-positioning-and-audience.md)) you will not get past the first meeting.

- [Y Combinator Library](https://www.ycombinator.com/library) — free and canonical on how much to raise, how to run a process, and how to talk to investors. The single best free resource here.
- [Y Combinator standard legal documents](https://www.ycombinator.com/documents) — the actual SAFE templates and user guide. Read them before you negotiate anything.
- [Sequoia on writing a business plan](https://www.sequoiacap.com/article/writing-a-business-plan/) — the deck structure most seed decks descend from.
- [Venture Deals](https://www.venturedeals.com/) — Feld and Mendelson. The standard reference on how term sheets actually work. Read it before you need it.

## 28.2 The metrics pack

Investors check a consistent set of numbers, and being unable to produce them quickly is itself a
signal. Definitions are in [chapter 13](13-pricing-and-business-model.md); the sources:

- [16 Startup Metrics](https://a16z.com/16-startup-metrics/) and [16 More Startup Metrics](https://a16z.com/16-more-startup-metrics/) — a16z's reference definitions. Useful mainly so you and the investor mean the same thing by the same word.
- [SaaS Metrics 2.0](https://www.forentrepreneurs.com/saas-metrics-2/) — David Skok's long-standing reference on the underlying model.
- [The Dangerous Seduction of the Lifetime Value (LTV) Formula](https://abovethecrowd.com/2012/09/04/the-dangerous-seduction-of-the-lifetime-value-ltv-formula/) — Bill Gurley on why LTV is the most abused number in the category. Read this before you put an LTV:CAC ratio on a slide.
- [The Burn Multiple](https://sacks.substack.com/p/the-burn-multiple) — David Sacks. Now a standard efficiency check.
- [Bessemer State of the Cloud](https://www.bvp.com/atlas) and [SaaS Capital research](https://www.saas-capital.com/research/) — benchmarks. SaaS Capital is the more relevant of the two if you're bootstrapped or bootstrapped-adjacent.
- [Carta data](https://carta.com/data/) — original data on real round sizes, valuations and dilution. The best antidote to the survivorship bias in press coverage of funding.

**Two rules that matter more than any individual metric:** define every number the same way in
every document, and never present a metric you can't reproduce from source data on request. Being
caught with a flattering definition is worse than a mediocre number.

## 28.3 The deck, and the data room

The deck is a marketing artefact. It gets forwarded, skimmed on a phone, and read without you in
the room — so it must work unaccompanied.

- **One idea per slide**, with the idea in the headline rather than the body.
- **Ten to fifteen slides.** The appendix is where detail lives.
- **Readable at thumbnail size.**
- **Send a PDF.** Not a link that requires signup, unless you specifically want view tracking and accept the friction it adds.

- [DocSend startup pitch deck research](https://www.docsend.com/index/startup-pitch-deck/) — aggregate data on how investors actually read decks, including time spent per slide. Vendor research, but the underlying dataset is real and the findings are actionable.

**The data room** should exist before you need it: incorporation documents, cap table, financials,
key contracts, IP assignments, and your metrics with their definitions. Assembling this under time
pressure during diligence is how deals slow down and die.

## 28.4 Analysts, and whether they're worth it

**For most small technical vendors: not yet.** Gartner and Forrester influence enterprise buying,
but the programmes that get you meaningfully covered are priced for companies with real revenue,
and the effort is substantial.

**What is worth doing early**, because it's free or cheap:

- **[Gartner Peer Insights](https://www.gartner.com/reviews/home)** — free for vendors to claim a profile and gather reviews. The most credible of the review platforms (see [chapter 24](24-case-studies-and-social-proof.md)).
- **A vendor briefing** — analysts take briefings from vendors they don't cover, and it's a legitimate way onto the radar. [Gartner's briefing process](https://www.gartner.com/en/about/policies/vendor-briefings) is documented publicly.

- [IIAR>](https://analystrelations.org/) — the independent professional body for analyst relations. Useful for understanding what the discipline actually involves before you buy into it.

## 28.5 Press for technical companies

Most startup press advice is written for consumer companies and doesn't transfer. For a technical
product:

- **Trade press beats mainstream press.** A piece in a publication your buyers actually read outperforms a general-interest mention by a wide margin.
- **Funding announcements are the weakest kind of coverage.** They reach other founders and recruiters, not customers.
- **Your own technical writing frequently outperforms earned media.** A good engineering post can reach more of the right people than a press hit, and you control it. See [chapter 3](03-content-marketing.md).
- **Build relationships with two or three journalists** who cover your space, before you need something. Read their work, be a useful source on stories that aren't about you.

**Journalist sourcing after HARO.** HARO — the long-running service connecting journalists with
expert sources — was folded into Connectively, which subsequently shut down. Several successors
now occupy the space:

- [Qwoted](https://www.qwoted.com/) — journalist-source matching, freemium for sources. Generally regarded as the strongest of the replacements.
- [Featured](https://www.featured.com/) — question-and-answer sourcing, formerly Terkel.
- [Help a B2B Writer](https://www.helpab2bwriter.com/) — free and B2B-specific. Narrower and higher-signal than the general services, which makes it a good fit for this audience.
- [Source of Sources](https://www.sourceofsources.com/) — launched by HARO's original founder.

**Verify current status before investing time in any of these** — this category has churned
repeatedly and a service that was thriving a year ago may not be.

## 28.6 When things go wrong

Crisis communication is a marketing discipline and the one most likely to be improvised badly.

The rules are consistent across every credible framework: **be first, be right, be credible, be
empathetic, and say what you're doing about it.** Speed matters more than polish; a holding
statement in twenty minutes beats a perfect one in six hours.

For a technical company the most common crisis is an outage or a security incident, which means
[chapter 21](21-trust-and-compliance-as-marketing.md) is the operational half of this section.

- [CDC Crisis and Emergency Risk Communication](https://emergency.cdc.gov/cerc/) — free, public domain, evidence-based, and the best crisis-communication training material available at any price. Written for public health, transfers almost entirely.
- [Postmortem Culture: Learning from Failure](https://sre.google/sre-book/postmortem-culture/) — the blameless postmortem chapter from Google's SRE book.
- [danluu/post-mortems](https://github.com/danluu/post-mortems) — a large curated collection of real public postmortems. Read a few before you have to write one.

**Never** delete the incident, quietly edit the timeline, or blame a named employee in public.
Each of those turns a recoverable incident into a lasting reputation problem.

---

## 28.7 If you only do five things

1. Answer "why now" convincingly before you write any other slide.
2. Define every metric once, in writing, and use that definition everywhere.
3. Assemble the data room before you start raising.
4. Build relationships with two journalists who cover your space, before you need them.
5. Write your incident-communication template now, while nothing is on fire.
